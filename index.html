Signal ≠ Value Labv2.1 · Nov 2026

Two Point Singularity · Companion to Signal ≠ Value (Rao, 2026)

What people signal is not always what they value.

AI systems learn from clicks, ratings and preferences as if each were a direct readout of what a person wants. This lab separates what is known about that gap, what is being tested, what a full simulation of the test shows, and what is only proposed.

The one open questionWhen people judge under pressure, do their choices drift away from what they themselves choose on reflection, in a consistent direction rather than at random?

What would show this is wrong

  1. H1 is not supported if, in the registered study of about 60 people, the average shift toward the attractor is not reliably above zero. If its confidence interval lies below 3 percentage points, any effect is too small to matter by the study's own standard. Either result is published.
  2. H2 fails if no state signal collected in ordinary sessions reduces the lean of the training signal by at least a quarter, beyond what existing rater-quality methods achieve, while meeting prespecified criteria for every group.
  3. The proposal fails if improving working conditions alone does as well as weighting. The simulation suggests this is a live possibility, and the proposal has been changed to put it first.

Not only annotators

Every recorded digital action is a signal: a thumbs-up, an Arena vote, an upvote, a click, an "accept". Several of these demonstrably reach AI training. The study tests the cleanest case, paid and attentive raters; sets out what a result would and would not mean for everything else.

Why it matters

Noise averages out. Direction does not.

If rushed or tired people err at random, more feedback cancels the errors. If they err consistently toward confident-sounding or agreeable answers, more feedback teaches a system that lean more firmly. Published studies show raters favour such answers; none has tested whether the lean grows with the rater's state within the same person.

How far a result travels

  1. The general claim is established. Every recorded action is a signal produced under conditions, not a readout of value. It covers every channel above.
  2. State is one cause of eight, and in the wild rarely the largest. Defaults, design and incentives dominate several channels.
  3. A fortiori, not proof. The study tests the most favourable conditions: paid, attentive adults with a correct answer available. Wild signals come from phones, multitasking, late nights and interfaces built to get a click. If a lean appears in the favourable case, the assumption that it is absent in worse ones loses its basis.
  4. The asymmetry. A positive H1 widens the case for recording and auditing every human signal used in training. A precise null would constrain this mechanism in this setting, but would not settle the other seven causes.

What a 3-point lean means

In pairwise data, 53 instead of 50 is a learned reward difference of about 0.12 logits under the Bradley–Terry model most reward models use. Across one million comparisons it is about 30,000 extra choices in one direction. Optimising against a learned reward amplifies systematic errors in it (Gao et al., 2023). Whether this size of lean changes model behaviour is untested (H6).

The line this lab does not cross

For end-user signals, CWHF applies only in aggregate: record how and when feedback was asked for, stop asking at moments of high load, and audit the style lean of the signal before training on it. Inferring an individual's state from their behaviour, and using it to decide what they are shown, is outside CWHF and contrary to its conditions of use.

Hypothesis · demonstration

The flip test

You answer 12 questions twice: once under pressure, with a timer set to your own reading speed while you hold a number in mind, and once at your own pace. Each question has two answers, one more confident or more agreeable than the other. The lab measures one thing: did pressure shift your choices toward that style?

A demonstration of the method, not research participation and not research data. It says nothing about your values, health or abilities. Nothing you do here leaves your browser. The order of the two rounds is chosen at random, as in the real study.

How this demo differs from the study

  • 12 questions instead of 48, and one person instead of 60.
  • Both rounds happen minutes apart. In the simulation, memory between rounds hid more than half of a real effect, so the study separates sessions by at least a week.
  • Questions with a "longer answer" style are left out. The simulation showed a timer cannot test length fairly: you cannot choose what you cannot read.
  • The timer is set from your own reading speed on two practice questions, so slower readers are not simply timed out.

The computation

For each round, count how often you chose the attractor (the more confident or more agreeable answer). The attractor is the correct answer in exactly half the questions, so a shift toward it is not just a shift toward mistakes.

D = attractor rate (rushed) − attractor rate (own pace)

D near zero means pressure made you noisier but not one-sided. A positive D means pressure pulled you toward the style. The study tests whether the average D across 60 people is above zero and at least 3 percentage points.

Simulated · about the design, not about people

The study, run on simulated participants

Before any real person takes part, the full H1 and H2 studies were run thousands of times on simulated people whose true behaviour was set in advance: sometimes with no drift at all, sometimes with small, moderate or very small drift. That shows whether the design finds drift when it is there and stays quiet when it is not.

What it cannot show: whether real people drift. Every result here follows from assumptions about how people behave. Those assumptions are listed in the paper and its technical supplement, and the code is open.

Minimum viable study

60 people, 48 questions, two sessions a week apart

Significant for a 5–6 point drift in at least 99% of simulated studies, and for a 3.5-point drift in about 80%; significant and at least 3 points in 96% and 48% respectively. Assumes the spread of D across people is near the simulated 8.5 points (see Method). At 40 people, the 5–6 point detection rate is still about 97%.

False alarms

5.3% when there is no drift

Across 2,000 simulated studies with no drift, the pre-registered test fired 5.3% of the time, matching its 5% design. With the 3-point minimum added, false confirmations fall to 0.4%.

Design flaws found

Four, all fixed

The 0.60 threshold, the timed length questions, same-sitting rounds and a slightly over-eager statistical test. Details below.

H2 · Does weighting remove the lean from the training signal?

Scenario
MethodRemaining lean (pp)Lean removedData kept (ESS)Weight of 2nd-language readers

Lean = how far the labels sit from the reflective population, toward the attractor, in percentage points. ESS = effective sample size as a share of all labels. Weight ratio below 0.90 is flagged as an equity breach. "Oracle" weights know each session's true state, so they mark the ceiling for any weighting.

H2 · What this means

  • The gentle v1 weight formula removes under 10% of the lean with a realistic state signal, and about a quarter even with perfect knowledge of every rater's state.
  • Sharper weights remove more, roughly 20–55%, but discard 35–55% of the effective data.
  • Scores built from raw response times cut second-language readers' weight to between 0.4 and 0.7. Comparing each person only with their own baseline removes that breach.
  • Improving conditions at source (shorter sessions, breaks) removed about 30% of the lean with no data lost and no group penalised.

What changed because of it

  • The proposal now puts prevention first and weighting third.
  • H2 compares weighting against a working-conditions fix, not only against other weighting methods.
  • The weight formula is treated as a parameter H2 must choose, not as a default.
  • Within-person scoring is mandatory, even though it cannot correct people who are depleted all the time. That trade-off is stated, not hidden.
Proposed · depends on H1 passing

CWHF v2: record, prevent, weight, audit

If H1 holds, human feedback should be treated like any measurement taken under conditions: the conditions are recorded, bad conditions are avoided, and what cannot be avoided is corrected and audited. Version 1 jumped straight to weighting. The simulation showed that order was wrong. CWHF now names the programme, not a formula; "coherence" means how closely a session agrees with the same person's reflective choices.

1 · Record

Log the conditions

A 30-second check-in, session length, time of day and position in the session, stored next to each label. Cheap, and useful even if nothing else is adopted.

2 · Prevent

Fix conditions at source

Session caps, breaks, scheduling and per-hour rather than per-item pay. In simulation, under its stated assumptions, this removed about 30% of the lean with no data lost.

3 · Weight

Down-weight only clear cases

Within-person scores only. The weight function is chosen in H2, where it must beat existing methods.

4 · Audit

Publish the effects

Report the lean, the data kept and every group's average weight, before and after.

The model, stated conditionally

If a share p of feedback sessions is reflective and the rest carry an average lean δ, a reward model trained with equal weights learns roughly:

r̂ ≈ r* + (1 − p) · δ

If δ averages to zero, more data removes the error. If it does not, more data entrenches it. This is a model, not a theorem: whether δ is above zero is exactly what H1 tests.

Two candidate weight functions, both untested

Gentle = the v1 formula. Sharp = a steeper alternative. In simulation, gentle weights barely moved the lean and sharp weights halved the data. H2 chooses between them, or neither.

Adopt now, before any result

  • Record session length, position, time of day and pay model with every label.
  • State collection conditions in every dataset card.
  • Avoid per-item pay and long unbroken queues.
  • Audit the style of responses that win product feedback, early versus late in sessions, before training on it.

Governance of step 1

  • Purpose: data quality only.
  • Stored apart from identity; deleted or aggregated within a stated period.
  • Named access, every access logged.
  • Raters can see their records and decline the check-in.
  • Reported only in aggregate.

If H2 fails

The recommendation becomes record, prevent and audit, with no weighting. That is a useful result: it tells labs where not to spend effort.

Predicted adoption: recording first, because it is cheap; prevention deferred, because it costs. The audit step is what makes the deferral visible.

Conditions of use

  • Self-report and task signals only. No webcam, wearable or biometric inference of anyone's emotional state.
  • State scores never affect a rater's pay, ranking, hiring or continued work.
  • Weights compare each person only with their own baseline. Raw scores penalised second-language readers in simulation.
  • Published audits of weight distributions by region, shift and language, with a breach threshold of 0.90.
  • Informed consent and opt-out; state data stored apart from identity and deleted after use.
  • No use of the method to detect and target people when they are depleted.
  • Scope: annotation and opt-in feedback only, until a consent model exists for anything wider. For end-user signals, aggregate audits only, never individual targeting.

Terms are used in one sense only, everywhere in this lab and in the whitepaper.

Design safeguards added in the final review

  • Attention checks: two instructed-response items per session, excluded from D.
  • Reasons on half the items: writing a reason can itself shift choices (Shafir, Simonson & Tversky, 1993), so it is asked on a random half of reflective items and its effect estimated.
  • Funnel debrief: participants who guess the attractor hypothesis are flagged and excluded in a sensitivity analysis.
  • Reference control arm: 12 extra people do two reflective sessions a week apart, measuring how much the reference itself moves.
  • Timeouts by group: reported by first language and age, because simulated second-language readers timed out on about a third of pressured items.
  • Order × condition interaction pre-registered.

The normative choice, stated

Treating the reflective choice as the reference is a choice, not a discovery. It follows a long tradition of giving weight to considered over momentary preferences, and it can be wrong: reflection is sometimes worse (see the counterexamples in Evidence). The test asks only whether the gap has a direction, which needs no view about which self is "real".

CWHF also measures reliability, not goodness. A calm, consistent judgment can still be harmful, so content safety must sit alongside it.

What is new, and what is not

  • Not new: that signals differ from preferences (behavioural welfare economics); that raters favour agreeable, assertive and long answers (Sharma et al. 2023, Hosking et al. 2023, Singhal et al. 2023); that judgments mix stable traits and passing states (latent state-trait theory); weighting raters by reliability (Dawid–Skene).
  • Closest prior work: Kopteva & Hlynianyi-Zhuk, 2026 in "Rater State Bias in RLHF Preference Data: An Audit Framework" study sustained stress over time, an output-side signature and an audit of public models. This programme manipulates acute pressure within person and measures the input side. Complementary.
  • New here: using each person's own reflective choice as the reference; the D test with an attractor crossed with correctness; session-level, within-person weighting; and a simulation showing when weighting can and cannot help.
