Signal ≠ Value

Does pressure bend the human feedback that trains AI?
A testable question, a study design simulated before any data, and a revised programme for Coherence-Weighted Human Feedback (CWHF).

At a glance

The whole paper on one page

The question

When people judge under pressure, do their choices drift away from what they themselves choose on reflection, in a consistent direction rather than at random?

Why it matters

Random error cancels as more feedback is collected. A consistent lean does not: more data teaches an AI system the lean more firmly.

What is known

Judgments shift with fatigue and time pressure. Raters of AI output favour agreeable, assertive and longer answers. User thumbs data has already pushed one deployed model toward sycophancy.

What is not known

Whether the lean grows when the same person is under pressure. We have not found a study that tests this within-person contrast directly. Kopteva and Hlynianyi-Zhuk (2026) propose rater state as a structured bias, with an observational audit; this paper adds a pre-registered manipulation that can show whether the bias has a direction.

The test

Sixty people answer 48 questions twice, a week apart: once rushed, once at their own pace. One measure, D, says whether pressure moved them toward a pre-named style.

What the simulation found

Under its stated assumptions, the design catches a 6-point drift 99.7% of the time and raises a false alarm 5.3% of the time. It also exposed four flaws in the first paper's design, now fixed.

What we propose

Record the conditions of every label, prevent drift at source, weight only where it demonstrably helps, audit always. In simulation, fixing conditions beat every weighting formula.

What would prove us wrong

If the average shift is not reliably above zero, H1 is not supported; if its confidence interval lies below 3 points, any effect is too small to matter by our own standard. Either result is published.

How to read the claims. Every claim carries one of five labels, and no lower tier is used to prop up a higher one: Established published evidence; Hypothesis precise and testable, not yet tested; Simulated a property of the study design, shown on simulated people; Proposed depends on the hypotheses passing; Retired claimed in the first paper and withdrawn.
What this paper does not claim. It does not identify anyone's true values. It tests whether the conditions under which feedback is given shift the choices people make.

01A thumbs-up is not the value

Every science records the conditions of a measurement, because the instrument's state affects the reading. Human feedback to AI is the exception: a rating is stored as if it were a fact about the world, not an observation made by a particular person, at a particular moment, in a particular state.

A tired annotator at the end of a long queue, a parent tapping a thumbs-up at 2 a.m., a voter on a leaderboard between meetings: each produces a signal. Each signal passes through filters before it is recorded. The person's momentary state is one filter. Interface design, incentives, missing information, social pressure and the limits of the measurement itself are others.

The broader gap is long established. Behavioural economics stopped treating every choice as a revealed preference decades ago (Bernheim and Rangel, 2009). Engagement data diverges from what users say they want (Milli et al., 2023), and optimising for engagement under inconsistent preferences can leave people worse off (Kleinberg et al., 2022).

State is singled out here not because it is the largest filter. It usually is not. It is singled out because it varies within the same person, can be manipulated ethically in a study, and is cheap to record.

That means

A label is a measurement with a hidden condition attached. Today that condition is thrown away before training begins.

So what

If the hidden condition pushes labels one way, no amount of extra data will reveal it, because nobody recorded it.

02Every digital action is a signal

Paid annotators are only one source. Some everyday digital actions are already collected and used to train or evaluate AI systems; many more could be. The table lists channels where that use is documented, and one where practice is undisclosed.

The general claim is established: every recorded action is a signal produced under conditions. The state-specific claim is not. In the wild, defaults, design and incentives often dominate, and a claim that "state explains the gap everywhere" would overreach.

What links the two is an argument a fortiori. The study in this paper tests the most favourable conditions: paid, instructed, attentive adults choosing between two short answers, one of them correct. Signals in the wild come from phones, multitasking, late nights and interfaces built to obtain a click. If a lean appears under favourable conditions, the default assumption that it is absent under worse ones loses its basis. That is plausibility, not proof. The opposite is also possible: in the wild, design and incentives may swamp state altogether. That is why each channel needs its own test (Section 9).

That means

The question reaches every person whose clicks, votes and ratings feed an AI system, not only the people paid to label data.

So what

A positive result widens the case for recording conditions everywhere. A precise null would constrain this mechanism in this setting, but would not settle the other seven sources of divergence.

03Noise averages out. Direction does not.

The whole argument turns on one distinction. If pressure only adds random error, collecting more feedback cancels it. If pressure pushes choices consistently toward one style, more feedback teaches that style more firmly.

Suppose a share p of labelling sessions is effectively reflective and the rest carry an average lean δ toward a particular style. A reward model trained with equal weights learns, to a first approximation:

r̂ ≈ r* + (1 − p) · δ

Here r* is what it would learn from reflective labels alone. This is arithmetic conditional on the hypothesis, not a theorem about the world. The first paper called it the "Bias Theorem"; that name is retired.

What a 3-point lean means in practice

In pairwise data, 53 choices in 100 instead of 50 is a learned reward difference of about 0.12 on the logit scale used by most reward models, applied wherever the style appears. Across a million comparisons it is about 30,000 extra choices in one direction. Optimising a model against a learned reward is known to amplify systematic errors in that reward (Gao et al., 2023). Whether a lean of this size changes behaviour enough to matter is itself untested.

Why three points

The threshold is a practical convention, fixed before any data. It is roughly the smallest shift that is a stable reward difference of about 0.12 logits, adds up to tens of thousands of one-directional labels in a typical preference dataset, and can be detected with about 80% power at a feasible sample size. It can be challenged. The full confidence interval will be reported, so readers can apply their own threshold.

That means

The useful question is not "are tired people noisier?" (they are) but "does tiredness have a direction?"

So what

If it does, scale stops being a cure and becomes an amplifier. That is the cost of not knowing.

04What we already know

Five findings motivate the question. None of them is the question.

#FindingEvidenceWhat it does not show
E1Professional judgments shift with state, in one directionAntibiotic prescribing rose across clinic sessions, from adjusted odds 1.01 in the second hour to 1.26 in the fourth (Linder et al., 2014). Test scores fall later in the day and recover after breaks (Sievertsen et al., 2016)That AI raters behave the same way
E2Momentary state distorts choices people later reverseA 30°F colder order day raised cold-weather catalogue returns by about 3.95 points (Conlin et al., 2007)That reversal reflects reflection rather than new information
E3Raters of AI output favour certain stylesSycophantic answers preferred over correct ones a non-negligible share of the time (Sharma et al., 2023); assertiveness hides factual errors (Hosking et al., 2023); much reward gain tracks length (Singhal et al., 2023)That these preferences grow with rater state
E4Public preference data record no rater stateHH-RLHF, SHP and Chatbot Arena releases include no state field. Internal practice is not publicThat recording state would change outcomes
E5End-user feedback can move a deployed modelThumbs data contributed to the sycophantic GPT-4o update (OpenAI, 2025)Why the users leaned

The closest prior work is Kopteva and Hlynianyi-Zhuk (2026), who propose rater state as a structured bias in preference data, with an observational audit framework for public models. Their focus is sustained stress over time and an output-side signature. This paper works on the input side: it manipulates acute pressure within the same person and measures whether the shift has a direction. The two are complementary, and arrived at the question independently: our first paper (March 2026) and their first version (April 2026) appeared within weeks of each other.

One nearby literature urges caution. "Ego depletion", the idea that self-control draws on a limited resource, was not supported by two large pre-registered multi-site replications (Hagger et al., 2016; Vohs et al., 2021). The hypothesis here does not rely on a resource model. It predicts only that pressure changes which answer is chosen, whatever the mechanism, and the study uses direct, reversible manipulations with a check that they worked.

So what

The ingredients are all documented: state moves judgment, raters have style preferences, pipelines ignore state, and user feedback reaches reward models. What is missing is the one measurement that joins them.

05The question, stated so it can fail

A claim that cannot fail is not a scientific claim. This section fixes the words, the measure and the threshold before any data exist.

H1 · Hypothesis

Within the same person, judging under time pressure and divided attention increases the rate at which a pre-named "attractor" style is chosen, relative to that person's reflective choices on the same items, by at least 3 percentage points on average.

H2 · Hypothesis, run only if H1 holds

In ordinary labelling, a within-person session score reduces the lean of a training set by at least 25%, more than the best existing rater-quality method, while keeping at least 60% of effective data and meeting prespecified non-inferiority criteria for every language, region and shift group, with uncertainty reported.

H3 · Exploratory, observational

In public Chatbot Arena votes, the same voter's choices lean more toward the confident, agreeable or longer answer as a voting sitting wears on.

Five terms, one meaning each

Reflective reference choice
the "value" of the title		The choice the same person makes on the same item with no time limit and no distraction, after time to reflect. A convention chosen so the claim can be tested. The study tests condition-dependent preferences over answers; it does not identify moral values, authentic identity or anyone's uniquely correct preference. Reflection is sometimes worse (Wilson and Schooler, 1991).
AttractorA pre-named answer style expected to gain under pressure: more confident (unhedged), or more agreeable with the asker. It is the correct answer in exactly half the items, so a shift in accuracy cannot pass for a shift in style.
D · primaryThe reflective–pressured choice gap: how often a person picks the attractor under pressure, minus how often they pick it on reflection. Signed: it measures direction.
M · secondaryMismatch: the share of items where a person's two choices differ, in either direction. Five switches toward the attractor and five away give D = 0 but M > 0, so the two answer different questions.
CoherenceThe programme's interpretation, not a measure: how closely a session's choices agree with the same person's reflective choices. The study measures D and M; "coherence" names what they are about.
That means

"Value" is not a metaphysical claim. It is a reference point anyone can measure and anyone can dispute.

So what

A directional gap biases training data whichever self one prefers, because the data average to neither.

06The flip test

Study 1 compares each person with themselves. Sixty adults on a research platform, paid by the hour, answer the same 48 question pairs twice, at least a week apart, in counterbalanced order.

Built-in safeguards

  • Length removed from the timed test. Long answers go unread under a timer, which would fake a result. Length is tested separately, without a timer (Study 3).
  • A bundled manipulation, stated as such. The pressured session combines a timer and a memory load, so Study 1 estimates their combined effect. A later 2 × 2 study (timer × load) can separate them.
  • Reasons on half the items. Writing a reason can itself shift choices (Shafir et al., 1993). The primary D uses all items; a pre-registered sensitivity analysis uses only items with no written reason.
  • Carry-over, measured rather than assumed away. Item order is randomised in each session, and the debrief asks whether people remembered earlier answers. Seven days reduces memory effects but does not remove them; the control arm and an order × condition test estimate what remains.
  • Attention checks, a manipulation check and a funnel debrief that flags anyone who guessed the hypothesis.
  • A reference control arm: twelve extra people do two reflective sessions, measuring how much the reference itself moves.
  • Timeouts reported by first language and age, because second-language readers are most affected by a timer.

The decision rule, fixed in advance

Significance and size are judged separately, from the mean of D and its 95% confidence interval.

OutcomeRuleWhat it means
Exceeds the thresholdLower bound above 3 pointsThe effect is larger than the practical threshold

That means

Every person is their own control, so stable differences in skill, language or temperament are controlled. They may still change how strongly pressure acts.

So what

The study costs about $3,000–7,000 and eight to ten weeks, and every possible result is publishable.

07Testing the test

Before spending money on people, the whole study was run thousands of times on simulated participants drawn from eight personas: a rested specialist, a gig annotator paid per item, a night-shift health worker, a second-language reader, a student, a retiree, an overconfident generalist and an anxious perfectionist.

Four flaws the simulation caught in the first paper's design

FlawWhat the simulation showedFix
Pass rule "π ≥ 0.60"Missed a real 6-point drift in 79% of studiesTest the average drift D directly
Both sessions on one dayMemory cut the measured drift by more than halfSessions at least 7 days apart
Fixed 7-second timer with long answersReversed the sign of the resultPersonal timer; length tested untimed
Item-level corrected estimatorFalse alarms 9.5% of the timeSimple person-level test: 5.3%

Two chances, reported separately. For a true drift of about 3.5 points, the test is significant in 80% of simulated studies but significant and with a mean of at least 3 points in only 48%, because the measured shift runs below the true one. For about 6 points the figures are 99.7% and 96%; for about 1.7 points, 36% and 12%.

One honest caveat. Power depends on how much D varies between people. The simulation gave a spread of about 8.5 points. If it is nearer 11, sixty people give only about 69% power for a 3-point drift. The study therefore pre-registers a rule: after the first 20 people, an analyst who cannot see the average computes the standard deviation of D and sets the final sample, between 60 and 100, from a formula fixed in the registration. Every simulated result can be reproduced from the published code and seeds (H1: 400–2,000 replications per setting; H2: 80 per scenario).

That means

The design was broken in four places, and the simulation found all four before a single participant was paid.

So what

Publishing the simulation and its code before the data is what makes the eventual result, whichever way it falls, credible.

08What to do about it

The first paper's answer was a weighting formula. The simulation of Study 2 changed that answer.

CWHF now names a programme, not a formula. It runs in four steps, cheapest first:

StepWhat it doesDepends on
1 · RecordStore session length, position, time of day, pay model and a two-item check-in with every labelNothing. Useful even if H1 fails
2 · PreventCap sessions, enforce breaks, pay by the hour, keep hard items away from the end of long queuesH1, to justify the cost
3 · WeightDown-weight a person's own worst sessions, never comparing people with each otherH2 passing all its conditions
4 · AuditPublish the lean, the data kept and every group's weight for each training setSteps 1–3

Adopt now, before any result: record conditions with every label, state collection conditions in every dataset card, avoid per-item pay and long unbroken queues, and compare the style of answers that win product feedback early versus late in sessions before training on it.

What a lab gets in the first quarter. Honestly, nothing measurable until H1 and H2 report. What makes it worth starting anyway: step 1 is a handful of logging fields most platforms already hold, and a retrospective check of existing thumbs data against session position (H4) needs days, not a study. For high-risk systems, Article 10 of the EU AI Act requires data governance that includes examining possible biases; condition records and step-4 audits could support that documentation. That is a possible use, not a compliance claim.

Rules for step 1. A log of anyone's state is a surveillance risk in its own right. It is kept for data quality only, stored apart from identity, deleted or aggregated within a stated period, accessed by a named few with every access logged, visible to the rater, and reported only in aggregate. It never affects pay, ranking or continued work.

Two known weak points. If session scores ever influence weights, raters may learn to perform alertness and platforms may build shadow scores; scores are therefore never shown to managers or used per person, anchor items are harder to game than self-reports, and audits look for gaming. And comparing people only with themselves has a blind spot: someone who is always depleted looks normal against their own baseline. Prevention, not weighting, is the remedy for that.

That means

Bad working conditions may be a data-quality problem, not only a welfare problem. In the simulation, the cheapest fix was also the fairest; Study 2 tests whether that holds in practice.

So what

Labs can start with step 1 tomorrow at almost no cost. If H2 fails, the recommendation simply becomes record, prevent and audit.

09How far one result travels

H1 is the first link in a chain of separately testable questions. Each needs its own data.

Study 3: the same question in public votes

Chatbot Arena's public release records, for each vote, the two models, both answers, the vote, an anonymised user ID and a timestamp. Study 3 groups each user's votes into sittings and asks whether the chance of choosing the more confident, more agreeable or longer answer rises with position in the sitting, comparing each voter only with themselves. It needs no new participants, only research time; it can start now; and it is the one place length can be tested fairly.

It is observational. Later votes differ from earlier ones in more ways than fatigue: topics drift within a sitting, model pairs vary in difficulty, and voters learn. The registration will list these confounds and the controls for each. A positive slope is a reason for H4, not evidence of cause. A flat slope in a large sample would count against the wider claim.

That means

The first real data on this question can come from a public dataset, before any lab study is funded.

So what

Developers who hold thumbs data with session metadata can run H4 in an afternoon. This paper is an invitation to do so.

10The strongest objections

Stated as a hostile reviewer would put them, with the answer this paper can give.

ObjectionAnswer
"The reflective choice is not the right reference."Agreed that it is a convention. The test only asks whether the gap has a direction, and a directional gap biases data whichever reference one prefers.
"You are extrapolating from 60 people to billions of users."No. The general claim is established separately; the step to wild signals is stated as plausibility, and each channel needs its own test.
"A lab task is not real labelling work."True. Study 1 tests whether the effect can exist; Study 2 tests whether it appears in ordinary sessions.
"Pressure might make people more accurate."Then D is near zero or negative and H1 fails. The design allows that outcome.
"Three points is too small to matter."Across a million labels it is 30,000 choices in one direction. The threshold is fixed in advance so it cannot move after the data.
"OpenAI already found and fixed sycophancy."That incident shows the pathway exists. It does not show why the signal leaned, or that other signals are free of it.
"Raters will game a state score."Possibly, if scores ever affect weights. Scores are never used per person or shown to managers, anchor items are harder to game than self-reports, and prevention needs no score at all.
"Kopteva and Hlynianyi-Zhuk already proposed this."They proposed rater state as a structured bias with an observational audit. This paper adds the experiment that can show whether pressure causes a directional shift. Complementary, and an invitation to collaborate.
"The simulation proves nothing."Correct. It shows the design works under stated assumptions. The real study will show whether the assumptions hold.

Further limits. Only two attractor styles are tested; the first study runs in one language; the items are constructed rather than real model outputs; and time pressure is not the same as fatigue at the end of a long shift. Each limit is a reason for a follow-up, named in the roadmap.

11Ethics and equity

The risk is less in the study than in what is done with its results.

The study. Both experimental studies run only after independent ethics approval. The manipulation is mild and reversible: a timer and a number to remember. Participants know one session will be rushed, are paid the same regardless of speed or accuracy, and can stop at any time.

Equity. A state score can easily become a proxy for who someone is: shift workers, carers, older people, second-language readers. The simulation showed this happens even with a perfect state measure. Hence three standing rules: compare each person only with themselves; protect every group's weight and outcome; and never let a score affect anyone's pay or work.

Beyond annotation. For end users, CWHF applies only in aggregate. Inferring an individual's state from their behaviour, and using it to decide what they are shown, is outside CWHF and contrary to its conditions of use. No webcam, wearable or biometric inference, ever.

Reliability is not goodness. A calm, consistent rater can still endorse a harmful answer. CWHF sits alongside content safety review; it never replaces it.

So what

The most effective remedy found, better working conditions, is also the one that benefits the people producing the data.

12What changed since the first paper

The first paper, The Coherence-Weighted Human Feedback (CWHF): Correcting the Foundational Flaw in AI Alignment (Rao, March 2026, doi:10.5281/zenodo.19235970), proposed the idea. This paper keeps its question, narrows it, tests the design, and withdraws what the evidence does not support. It is a new paper, not a revision, and where the two disagree this one prevails.

First paper claimedWhy withdrawnNow

That means

The idea survived its own audit. Most of the scaffolding around it did not.

So what

Retiring claims in public is the price of being taken seriously, and the strongest signal that the remaining ones are meant.

How to take part

  • Take the flip test in the companion lab at twopointsingularity.com/lab. It is a demonstration, not research participation, and says nothing about you.
  • Rerun the simulation. Code, seeds and outputs are open at github.com/venramak/twopointsingularity-cwhf. Change the assumptions and see whether the conclusions hold.
  • Review the items. Anyone who finds a question with two defensible answers improves the study directly.
  • Collaborate. University researchers who can provide ethics review, and teams who could record step 1 in their pipelines or run H4, are invited to write to venkat@twopointsingularity.com.

The question is small enough to test and large enough to matter. The next step is data.
