Signal ≠ Value

Does pressure bend the human feedback that trains AI?

At a glance

The whole paper on one page

The question

When people judge under pressure, do their choices move away from the choices the same people make on reflection, in a consistent direction rather than at random?

Why it matters

Random error cancels as more feedback is collected. A consistent lean does not: if it reaches training, more data teaches an AI system the lean more firmly.

What is known

Judgments shift with fatigue and time pressure. Raters of AI output favour agreeable, assertive and longer answers. User thumbs data has already pushed one deployed model toward sycophancy.

What is not known

Whether the lean grows when the same person is under pressure. We have not found a study that tests this within-person contrast directly. Kopteva and Hlynianyi-Zhuk (2026) propose rater state as a structured bias, with an observational audit; this paper adds a pre-registered manipulation that can show whether the bias has a direction.

The test

Sixty people answer 48 questions twice, a week apart: once rushed, once at their own pace. One measure, D, says whether pressure moved them toward a pre-named style.

What the simulation found

On simulated participants, under stated assumptions, the design detects a 6-point drift 99.7% of the time and raises a false alarm 5.3% of the time. It also exposed four flaws in the first paper's design, now fixed. No real person has yet been tested.

What we propose

Record the conditions of every label, prevent drift at source, weight only where it demonstrably helps, audit always. In simulation, fixing conditions removed about 30% of the lean with no data lost; the first paper's weighting formula removed about 7% (Figure 5).

What would prove us wrong

If the average shift is not reliably above zero, H1 is not supported; if its confidence interval lies below 3 points, any effect is too small to matter by our own standard. Either result is published.

How to read the claims. Every claim carries one of five labels, and no lower tier is used to prop up a higher one: Established published evidence; Hypothesis precise and testable, not yet tested; Simulated a property of the study design, shown on simulated people; Proposed depends on the hypotheses passing; Retired claimed in the first paper and withdrawn.
What this paper establishes
  • A testable question about the conditions of human feedback, with one primary estimand (D)
  • A within-person protocol and decision rules fixed before any data
  • A simulation of that protocol, with open code, that found and fixed four design flaws
  • A staged programme (record, prevent, weight, audit) and the evidence each step still needs
What it does not establish
  • That pressure shifts real AI feedback: no human data have been collected
  • That reflective choices are anyone's true values: they are a stated reference
  • That correcting a lean improves reward models or alignment (H6, untested)
  • That state is the largest source of divergence: usually it is not

Decision brief

For heads of data, alignment and trust & safety

The decision. Should we record the conditions under which each human label is made, before anyone knows whether those conditions bend the labels?

Why now. Public preference datasets carry no condition fields (Section 4). If H1 holds, a lean is already in the data and cannot be audited after the fact, because the conditions were never stored.

If H1 fails, the records still document how the data were made, which dataset cards and data-governance reviews increasingly ask for. If H1 holds, the records are what make the lean measurable and the remedy checkable.

Recommendation. Adopt step 1 (record) for new collection now; it needs no weighting, filtering or change in pay. Defer step 3 (weight) until H2 passes. Treat step 2 (prevent) as the remedy to test first if H1 holds, because in simulation it beat weighting.

01A thumbs-up is not the value

Every science records the conditions of a measurement, because the instrument's state affects the reading. Human feedback to AI is the exception: a rating is stored as if it were a fact about the world, not an observation made by a particular person, at a particular moment, in a particular state.

A tired annotator at the end of a long queue, a parent tapping a thumbs-up at 2 a.m., a voter on a leaderboard between meetings: each produces a signal. Each signal passes through filters before it is recorded. The person's momentary state is one filter. Interface design, incentives, missing information, social pressure and the limits of the measurement itself are others.

The eight are state, choice architecture, incentives, information gaps, time inconsistency, social pressure, preference construction, and proxy or measurement limits. They are a working taxonomy drawn from behavioural welfare economics and consumer research, grouped because each needs a different remedy. They are not claimed to be exhaustive or fully separate: social pressure and preference construction often co-occur, and an information gap can be a precondition for the others.

The broader gap is long established. Behavioural economics stopped treating every choice as a revealed preference decades ago (Bernheim and Rangel, 2009). Engagement data diverges from what users say they want (Milli et al., 2023), and optimising for engagement under inconsistent preferences can leave people worse off (Kleinberg et al., 2022).

State is singled out here not because it is the largest filter. It usually is not. It is singled out because it varies within the same person, can be manipulated ethically in a study, and is cheap to record.

That means

A label is a measurement with a hidden condition attached. Today that condition is thrown away before training begins.

So what

If the hidden condition pushes labels one way, no amount of extra data will reveal it, because nobody recorded it.

What is established: the first three channels demonstrably reach AI systems, and every recorded action is produced under some condition. The consent row is an example of the same structure, not a claim that cookie choices train models. The state-specific claim is not established. In the wild, defaults, design and incentives often dominate, and a claim that "state explains the gap everywhere" would overreach.

What links the two is an argument a fortiori. The study in this paper tests the most favourable conditions: paid, instructed, attentive adults choosing between two short answers, one of them correct. Signals in the wild come from phones, multitasking, late nights and interfaces built to obtain a click. If a lean appears under favourable conditions, the default assumption that it is absent under worse ones loses its basis. That is plausibility, not proof. The opposite is also possible: in the wild, design and incentives may swamp state altogether. That is why each channel needs its own test (Section 9).

So what

A positive H1 is a reason to test each channel, not a finding about any of them. A precise null would constrain this mechanism in this setting, but would not settle the other seven sources of divergence.

03Noise averages out. Direction does not.

The whole argument turns on one distinction. If pressure only adds random error, collecting more feedback cancels it. If pressure pushes choices consistently toward one style, more feedback teaches that style more firmly.

Suppose a share p of labelling sessions is effectively reflective and the rest carry an average lean δ toward a particular style. A reward model trained with equal weights learns, to a first approximation:

r̂ ≈ r* + (1 − p) · δ

Here r* is what it would learn from reflective labels alone. This is arithmetic conditional on the hypothesis, not a theorem about the world (the first paper's name for it is retired; Section 12). Methods that fit the preference pairs directly, such as direct preference optimisation (Rafailov et al., 2023), are not exempt: they learn whatever lean the pairs carry. A second-order risk follows if a model trained on leaned labels produces answers that attract more leaned feedback, so the lean compounds across deployments. That loop is stated as part of H6, not as a finding.

What a 3-point lean means in practice

In pairwise data, 53 choices in 100 instead of 50 is a learned reward difference of about 0.12 on the logit scale used by most reward models, applied wherever the style appears. In a dataset of a million pairwise comparisons, the order of public releases, it is about 30,000 extra choices in one direction. That figure assumes independent comparisons; choices by the same rater are correlated, so it is an upper bound on the information the lean carries, not an estimate. The direction of the argument does not depend on it. Optimising a model against a learned reward is known to amplify systematic errors in that reward (Gao et al., 2023). Whether a lean of this size changes behaviour enough to matter is itself untested.

Why three points

The threshold is a practical convention, fixed before any data, and it is partly tied to the design. Three things set it: a 3-point lean is a stable reward difference of about 0.12 logits; it adds up to many thousands of one-directional labels at dataset scale; and it is about the smallest drift sixty people can detect with 80% power if D varies across people as simulated (minimum detectable effect 2.8 points at a spread of 8.5; Section 7). The third reason makes the threshold partly a property of the study, and we say so rather than hide it. In standardised terms 3 points is d ≈ 0.35. A 1-point drift is also a drift; this study is simply not built to confirm one. The full confidence interval is reported, so readers can apply their own threshold (Lakens et al., 2018).

That means

The useful question is not "are tired people noisier?" (they are) but "does tiredness have a direction?"

So what

If it does, and the lean reaches training, scale stops being a cure and becomes an amplifier. That is the cost of not knowing.

One nearby literature urges caution. "Ego depletion", the idea that self-control draws on a limited resource, was not supported by two large pre-registered multi-site replications (Hagger et al., 2016; Vohs et al., 2021). The hypothesis here does not rely on a resource model. It predicts only that pressure changes which answer is chosen, whatever the mechanism, and the study uses direct, reversible manipulations with a check that they worked.

So what

The ingredients are all documented: state moves judgment, raters have style preferences, pipelines ignore state, and user feedback reaches reward models. What is missing is the one measurement that joins them.

05The question, stated so it can fail

A claim that cannot fail is not a scientific claim. This section fixes the words, the measure and the threshold before any data exist.

H1 · Hypothesis

Within the same person, judging under time pressure and divided attention increases the rate at which a pre-named "attractor" style is chosen, relative to that person's reflective choices on the same items, by at least 3 percentage points on average.

H2 · Hypothesis, run only if H1 holds

In ordinary labelling, a within-person session score reduces the lean of a training set by at least 25%, more than the best existing rater-quality method, while keeping at least 60% of effective data and meeting prespecified non-inferiority criteria for every language, region and shift group, with uncertainty reported.

H3 · Exploratory, observational

In public Chatbot Arena votes, the same voter's choices lean more toward the confident, agreeable or longer answer as a voting sitting wears on.

The estimand

For person i, over the ni primary items answered within time in both sessions, let Pi and Ri be the shares of choices for the attractor under pressure and on reflection. Let ui count items switched toward the attractor and wi items switched away. Then

Di = Pi − Ri = (ui − wi) / ni   ·   Mi = (ui + wi) / ni   ·   Δ = E[Di]

The identity matters. An item chosen the same way twice contributes nothing, so D counts only departures from the person's own reflective choice, signed by direction; M counts them unsigned. The target is Δ, the average within-person difference among adults like those recruited, on items like these, under this bundled pressure condition. Analysis population, timeouts and missing items are specified in Appendix A.

Six terms, one meaning each

Reflective reference choice
the "value" of the title		The choice the same person makes on the same item with no time limit and no distraction, after time to reflect. A convention chosen so the claim can be tested. The study tests condition-dependent preferences over answers; it does not identify moral values, authentic identity or anyone's uniquely correct preference. Reflection is sometimes worse (Wilson and Schooler, 1991).
AttractorA pre-named answer style expected to gain under pressure: more confident (unhedged), or more agreeable with the asker. It is the correct answer in exactly half the items, so a shift in accuracy cannot pass for a shift in style.
D · primaryThe reflective–pressured choice gap: how often a person picks the attractor under pressure, minus how often they pick it on reflection. Signed: it measures direction.
M · secondaryMismatch: the share of items where a person's two choices differ, in either direction. Five switches toward the attractor and five away give D = 0 but M > 0, so the two answer different questions.
Elicitation conditionThe circumstances of a choice that the study sets: timer, memory load, session order. What is manipulated. A person's inner state is not measured; only self-reported rushedness is checked.
CoherenceThe programme's interpretation, not a measure: how closely a session's choices agree with the same person's reflective choices. Methods and results use D and M only. "CWHF" now names the programme of Section 8; the first paper's weighting formula is one conditional step within it, not the brand. The name is kept for citation continuity.

"Value" in the title names the subject of the programme; in every technical statement it means the reflective reference choice. These definitions are used verbatim in the companion lab and the site glossary; any change raises the paper's version number.

So what

The test does not need reflection to be the better self. A directional gap biases training data whichever reference one prefers, because the data average to neither (Bernheim and Rangel, 2009).

06The flip test

Study 1 compares each person with themselves. Sixty adults on a research platform, paid by the hour, answer the same 48 question pairs twice, at least a week apart, in counterbalanced order.

A worked example. Participant P-027 answers 44 primary items within time in both sessions. On reflection she chose the attractor on 18 (40.9%). Under pressure she switched 7 items toward it and 3 away, so she chose it on 22 (50.0%). Her D is (7 − 3) / 44 = +9.1 points, and her M is 10 / 44 = 22.7%. H1 asks whether the average D across all participants is reliably above zero, and how large it is. One person's D says nothing about her as a rater.

That means

Every person is their own control, so stable differences in skill, language or temperament are controlled. They may still change how strongly pressure acts.

So what

The study costs about $3,000–7,000 at sixty people (up to about $11,000 at a hundred) and eight to ten weeks, and every possible result is publishable.

07Testing the test

Before spending money on people, the whole study was run thousands of times on simulated participants drawn from eight personas: a rested specialist, a gig annotator paid per item, a night-shift health worker, a second-language reader, a student, a retiree, an overconfident generalist and an anxious perfectionist.

Two chances, reported separately. For a true drift of about 3.5 points, the test is significant in 80% of simulated studies but significant and with a mean of at least 3 points in only 48%, because the measured shift runs below the true one. For about 6 points the figures are 99.7% and 96%; for about 1.7 points, 36% and 12%.

The sample-size rule, and a check that it is safe. After the first 20 analysable people, an analyst who receives only the standard deviation of D, never its mean, sets the final sample: the smallest number between 60 and 100 that gives 80% power for 3 points. The decision and its reason are logged on OSF before recruitment continues. If more than 100 would be needed, the study stops at 100 and reports the result as directional evidence at reduced power, with the calculation published for a follow-up. In simulation the full procedure kept the false-alarm rate at 4.9% (spread 8.5) and 4.8% (spread 11), against 5.0% for a fixed sample of 60, and raised the average sample to 82 when the spread was 11. Re-estimating a sample from variance alone is a standard internal-pilot design (Friede and Kieser, 2006).

Side bias, simulated. If pressure made each person favour one screen side by up to 25 points, randomising the attractor's side kept the false-alarm rate at 4.6% and the mean D at zero. Fixing the attractor on one side would have produced a false positive every time, which is why the randomisation is in the protocol.

Every simulated number in this paper maps to a script, a seed and an output file in the repository (Appendix B).

That means

The design was broken in four places, and the simulation found all four before a single participant was paid.

So what

Publishing the simulation and its code before the data is what makes the eventual result, whichever way it falls, credible.

08What to do about it

The first paper's answer was a weighting formula. The simulation of Study 2 changed that answer.

CWHF now names a programme, not a formula. It runs in four steps, cheapest first:

StepWhat it doesDepends on
1 · RecordStore session length, position, time of day, pay model and a two-item check-in with every label (schema in Appendix C)Nothing. Useful even if H1 fails
2 · PreventCap sessions, enforce breaks, pay by the hour, keep hard items away from the end of long queuesH1, to justify the cost
3 · WeightDown-weight a person's own worst sessions, never comparing people with each otherH2 passing all its conditions
4 · AuditPublish the lean, the data kept and every group's weight for each training setSteps 1–3

Adopt now, before any result: record conditions with every label, state collection conditions in every dataset card, avoid per-item pay and long unbroken queues, and compare the style of answers that win product feedback early versus late in sessions before training on it.

What a lab gets in the first quarter. Honestly, nothing measurable until H1 and H2 report. What makes it worth starting anyway: step 1 is a handful of logging fields most platforms already hold, and a retrospective check of existing thumbs data against session position (H4) needs days, not a study. For high-risk systems, Article 10 of the EU AI Act requires data governance that includes examining possible biases; condition records and step-4 audits could support that documentation. That is a possible use, not a compliance claim.

What each step costs. Step 1 costs a few logging fields and a retention policy. Step 2 is the expensive one: capped sessions and hourly pay lower throughput per rater. Step 3 costs almost nothing to compute and is only worth doing if H2 passes. Step 4 costs analyst time per training set. A lab that adopts step 1 now and defers step 2 is making a defensible choice, provided it publishes the deferral; that is what step 4 is for.

Rules for step 1, as a protocol. (1) Purpose: data quality only; never pay, ranking, discipline or continued work. (2) Separation: condition records keyed to a salted pseudonym, stored apart from identity. (3) Minimisation: only the Appendix C fields; no biometrics, webcam or wearable data. (4) Retention: raw records deleted or aggregated within a stated period. (5) Access: a named few, every access logged. (6) Reporting: aggregate only, with small groups suppressed. (7) Rights: raters may skip the check-in without penalty, see their own records, contest them and ask for deletion. A lab that will not accept these terms should not adopt step 1: a state log without them is worse than none.

How H2 will be judged. Each pass condition has an operational definition, fixed with the registration (full text in the technical supplement).

Two known weak points. If session scores ever influence weights, raters may learn to perform alertness and platforms may build shadow scores; scores are therefore never shown to managers or used per person, anchor items are harder to game than self-reports, and audits look for gaming. And comparing people only with themselves has a blind spot: someone who is always depleted looks normal against their own baseline. Prevention, not weighting, is the remedy for that.

That means

Bad working conditions may be a data-quality problem, not only a welfare problem. In the simulation, the cheapest fix was also the fairest; Study 2 tests whether that holds in practice.

So what

Labs can start with step 1 tomorrow at almost no cost. If H2 fails, the recommendation simply becomes record, prevent and audit.

09How far one result travels

H1 is the first link in a chain of separately testable questions. Each needs its own data.

Study 3: the same question in public votes

Chatbot Arena's public release records, for each vote, the two models, both answers, the vote, an anonymised user ID and a timestamp. Study 3 groups each user's votes into sittings and asks whether the chance of choosing the more confident, more agreeable or longer answer rises with position in the sitting, comparing each voter only with themselves. It needs no new participants, only research time; it can start now; and it is the one place length can be tested fairly.

Study 3 analysis plan in brief. Sitting: consecutive votes by one user ID with no gap over 30 minutes. Styles, fixed in advance and scored blind to position: difference between the two answers in hedging words (a published lexicon), in agreement with a view stated in the prompt (a classifier validated on 300 human-coded pairs) and in length. Model: logistic regression of choosing the higher-style answer on position in sitting, with user, model-pair and prompt-category fixed effects and standard errors clustered by user; one model per style; one-sided test of a positive slope, Holm-corrected across the three styles. Time of day is not used, because timestamps are not in voters' local time. Order of work: the analysis script is released with the registration, before the data are opened. Effort: a few weeks of one analyst's time.

It is observational. Later votes differ from earlier ones in more ways than fatigue: topics drift within a sitting, model pairs vary in difficulty, and voters learn. User fixed effects absorb who keeps voting; prompt-category effects absorb some topic drift; the rest is a stated limit. A positive slope is a reason for H4, not evidence of cause. A flat slope in a large sample would count against the wider claim.

What H6 would need. Comparable reward models or preference predictors trained on the same pairs under four regimes (original labels; labels with condition fields; labels from a source-fixed arm; condition-aware weighting), and evaluated on held-out reflective preferences, sensitivity to answer style, sycophancy evaluations, calibration and robustness across rater groups. A smaller lean in the data is not, by itself, better alignment; H6 is where that would be shown or not.

That means

Real, if observational, evidence on this question can come from a public dataset before any lab study is funded.

So what

Developers who hold thumbs data with timestamps and session IDs can run H4 with the Study 3 plan. The author offers to analyse such data jointly and to publish the result whichever way it falls.

10The strongest objections

Stated as a hostile reviewer would put them, with the answer this paper can give.

ObjectionAnswer
"The reflective choice is just another biased state, such as boredom or over-thinking."It may be. Policy evaluation always needs a stated reference (Bernheim and Rangel, 2009), and this one is chosen for testability. The test only asks whether the gap has a direction: if fast choices are the better ones, or neither is, a directional gap still means the data average to neither.
"You are extrapolating from 60 people to billions of users."No. The general claim is established separately; the step to wild signals is stated as plausibility, and each channel needs its own test.
"A lab task is not real labelling work."True. Study 1 tests whether the effect can exist; Study 2 tests whether it appears in ordinary sessions.
"Pressure might make people more accurate."Then D is near zero or negative and H1 fails. The design allows that outcome.
"Three points is too small to matter, or was picked to suit the sample."It is partly set by the design, as Section 3 says. It is fixed in advance, reported with the full interval, and anyone may apply their own threshold.
"This is just noise, which Kahneman and colleagues already described."Noise is variance, and averaging reduces it. The question here is whether the error has a sign, which averaging does not remove.
"Rushed people pick whatever is on one side."The attractor's side is randomised per item and person; in simulation, strong side bias produced no false positives beyond the 5% expected.
"Why not a field study of real shift boundaries?"It would have more external validity and no causal identification. Study 1 trades realism for a within-person causal estimate; Studies 2 and 3 restore the field side.
"A rater's preferences may legitimately change."A week apart they may. The control arm measures how far reflective choices move without pressure; D is read against that.
"OpenAI already found and fixed sycophancy."That incident shows the pathway exists. It does not show why the signal leaned, or that other signals are free of it.
"Raters will game a state score."Possibly, if scores ever affect weights. Scores are never used per person or shown to managers, anchor items are harder to game than self-reports, and prevention needs no score at all.
"Kopteva and Hlynianyi-Zhuk already proposed this."They proposed rater state as a structured bias with an observational audit. This paper adds the experiment that can show whether pressure causes a directional shift. Complementary, and an invitation to collaborate.
"The simulation proves nothing."Correct. It shows the design works under stated assumptions. The real study will show whether the assumptions hold.

Limits we acknowledge. Only two attractor styles are tested. The study runs in one language. The items are constructed, not real model outputs, and the 48 primary items are specified but not yet written; Study 1 can be no better than its item bank, so the full bank, reviewer notes and disputed items will be published with the data. Time pressure for twenty minutes is not fatigue at the end of a long shift. The manipulation check is self-reported. The author designed the analysis, so he will not analyse the confirmatory data unblinded, and the primary analysis script will be frozen before recruitment.

Pre-mortem: if this is wrong in three years, why?

  • The wrong attractor. In real labelling the style that gains under pressure may be shorter, safer or more hedged answers, not confident or agreeable ones. H1 could fail while a lean exists.
  • The wrong pressure. A timer and a memory load are not end-of-shift fatigue, money worries or loneliness. A lab effect may not generalise.
  • The wrong population. Paid participants who know they are in a study may behave differently from annotators paid by throughput.
  • Lab is not field. H1 can pass while H2 to H6 fail.
  • No problem to solve. Existing rater-quality methods, or the averaging inside reward-model training, may already absorb the effect. H2 and H6 test this; the paper does not assume otherwise.

If any of these proves true, the result is published, logged in the changelog, and the affected claim is narrowed or withdrawn, not defended.

11Ethics and equity

The risk is less in the study than in what is done with its results.

The study. Both experimental studies run only after independent ethics approval. The manipulation is mild and reversible: a timer and a number to remember. Participants know one session will be rushed, are paid the same regardless of speed or accuracy, and can stop at any time.

Equity. A state score can easily become a proxy for who someone is: shift workers, carers, older people, second-language readers. The simulation showed this happens even with a perfect state measure. Hence three standing rules: compare each person only with themselves; protect every group's weight and outcome; and never let a score affect anyone's pay or work.

Data protection. Condition records about identifiable raters are personal data under the GDPR and India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Step 1 therefore needs a lawful basis, purpose limitation, minimisation, stated retention and rights of access and deletion, and an adopter should say where the records are processed. This is a design requirement, not legal advice.

Dual use. A record of rater condition can be turned into a tool to screen, rank or drop workers, and nothing in the method prevents that. The conditions of use forbid it, but a condition of use is not enforcement. Adopters are asked to publish their recording policy, accept external audit of how records are used, and give raters the right to see and contest their own records.

Beyond annotation. For end users, CWHF applies only in aggregate. Inferring an individual's state from their behaviour, and using it to decide what they are shown, is outside CWHF and contrary to its conditions of use. No webcam, wearable or biometric inference, ever.

Reliability is not goodness. A calm, consistent rater can still endorse a harmful answer. CWHF sits alongside content safety review; it never replaces it.

For standards and policy readers. The programme proposes one auditable artefact: a conditions card attached to each training dataset, listing how labels were made (session lengths, pay model, check-in summaries), the measured lean, the data kept and every group's weight. It needs no disclosure of model internals and no new data from regulators; only that the conditions of human judgment be recorded where the label is made. It extends datasheets for datasets (Gebru et al., 2021) to the annotation layer. For high-risk systems, such a card could support the data-governance documentation of EU AI Act Article 10; that is a possible use, not a compliance claim.
So what

In simulation, the most effective remedy, better working conditions, is also the one that benefits the people producing the data. Whether that holds in practice is what Study 2 tests.

Appendix B

Simulation: model and reproducibility map

H1 model. For person i and item j, logit PR(attractor) = bi + ej + ai·clarj·Cj, and logit PP(attractor) = bi + ej + (1 − atti)·ai·clarj·Cj + pulli, with a lapse rate mixing in random choice under pressure. Cj = +1 where the attractor is correct, −1 otherwise. Skill a, lapse, attenuation att, susceptibility to pull, reading speed and style preference b vary by persona; all persona values are invented and listed in h1sim.py and the technical supplement.

H2 model. Raters label 600 items across sessions; each session has a depletion z in [0, 1]. Depletion raises lapses, lowers discrimination and, when a pull exists, moves choices toward the attractor. A session score combines a noisy two-item self-report with response-time variability. Lean is measured against the reflective population rate of each item. The source fix lowers mean depletion by 0.15 (sensitivity 0.05–0.20).
